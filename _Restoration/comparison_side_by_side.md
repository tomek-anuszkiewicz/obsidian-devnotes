| **The model may provide a plausible answer instead of revealing missing knowledge**<br><br>When the description is incomplete, an LLM tends to complete the story.<br><br>For example, it may implicitly assume that:<br><br>&bull; | **Constraints may come from business logic**<br><br>Technical constraints often originate in business requirements.<br><br>The reasoning chain should be:<br><br><pre><code>Business requirement<br>→ required system property<br>→ architectural constraint<br>→ technology choice</code></pre><br><br>Examples:<br><br><pre><code>Business rule:<br>A customer must never be charged twice.<br><br>Required property:<br>Duplicate execution must be safe or prevented.<br><br>Architectural consequence:<br>Idempotency, deduplication, transactional boundaries, or unique operation identifiers are required.</code></pre><br><br><pre><code>Business rule:<br>The user must immediately know whether a reservation succeeded.<br><br>Required property:<br>The result cannot rely only on eventual consistency.<br><br>Architectural consequence:<br>A purely asynchronous workflow may be insufficient.</code></pre><br><br><pre><code>Business rule:<br>The organization must reconstruct why a decision was made years later.<br><br>Required property:<br>Historical state and decision inputs must be preserved.<br><br>Architectural consequence:<br>Audit records, versioning, immutable logs, or event history may be required.</code></pre><br><br>A technology choice such as a database type may therefore be derived from the domain rather than being a purely technical preference.<br><br>At the same time, a stated constraint such as “we must use SQL Server” should be questioned.<br><br>It may represent:<br><br>&bull; a real organizational standard,<br><br>&bull; existing expertise,<br><br>&bull; licensing constraints,<br><br>&bull; integration dependencies,<br><br>&bull; transactional requirements,<br><br>&bull; direct reporting access,<br><br>&bull; | **The model may prefer solutions it can implement comfortably**<br><br>Even when the user asks a neutral question and does not suggest an answer, the resulting recommendation is not necessarily neutral.<br><br>An LLM tends to favor solutions that are:<br><br>&bull; common in its training data,<br><br>&bull; well documented,<br><br>&bull; represented by many public examples,<br><br>&bull; easy to explain using familiar patterns,<br><br>&bull; easy for the model to turn into plausible code.<br><br>The model does not have to consciously decide, “I will choose this because I can implement it.” The bias arises indirectly:<br><br><pre><code>Familiar and high-probability approach<br>→ proposed more often<br>→ justified more fluently<br>→ implemented more successfully by the same model</code></pre><br><br>This correlation is useful, because implementability matters. | **Earlier context can anchor the recommendation**<br><br>The bias can be triggered by merely mentioning a technology earlier in the conversation.<br><br>For example, if SQL Server, Kafka, Temporal, Kubernetes, microservices, or event sourcing appeared anywhere in the preceding discussion, the model may assign that technology more importance than it deserves. It may interpret the mention as:<br><br>&bull; an implicit preference,<br><br>&bull; an available part of the infrastructure,<br><br>&bull; a constraint that should be preserved,<br><br>&bull; evidence that the user expects the technology to be used,<br><br>&bull; or the intended direction of the conversation.<br><br>This can happen even when the technology was mentioned only as an example, comparison point, rejected idea, or unrelated background detail.<br><br>The effect is a form of contextual anchoring:<br><br><pre><code>Technology appears in the context<br>→ becomes more available during generation<br>→ shapes the alternatives and evaluation criteria<br>→ is more likely to be recommended</code></pre><br><br>Therefore, a neutral-sounding question asked after discussing a specific technology is not fully context-neutral. Derive the required system properties first, then generate alternatives without privileging technologies already present in the conversation.<br><br>A particularly risky workflow is:<br><br><pre><code>The model selects the criteria<br>→ selects the technology<br>→ justifies its own selection<br>→ implements it<br>→ reviews its own result</code></pre><br><br>The entire chain may be internally consistent while optimizing for an unverified interpretation of the problem. | **Named Semantics Are Better Than Silent Global Behavior**<br><br>Compare:<br><br><pre><code>await client.SendAsync(request);</code></pre><br><br>where retry is silently injected by global `HttpClient` configuration,<br><br>with:<br><br><pre><code>await request<br>    .Retry(RetryPolicies.ExternalRead)<br>    .Execute();</code></pre><br><br>The second version still uses abstraction.<br><br>However, the abstraction leaves a visible semantic trace.<br><br>The agent immediately knows that:<br><br><pre><code>this operation may execute more than once</code></pre><br><br>That knowledge can affect decisions about:<br><br>&bull; idempotency,<br><br>&bull; database writes,<br><br>&bull; external side effects,<br><br>&bull; request identifiers,<br><br>&bull; duplicate handling.<br><br>The exact implementation of retry remains reusable and centrally controlled. | *(End of restored note)* |
| **Large Applications Already Struggle With Truly Global Policies**<br><br>This approach may also address a problem that exists even without AI.<br><br>In a large system containing:<br><br><pre><code>hundreds of endpoints<br>many modules<br>multiple databases<br>different external integrations<br>different SLA requirements<br>different business risks</code></pre><br><br>a single global policy is rarely actually global.<br><br>It gradually becomes:<br><br><pre><code>default behavior<br>except Payments<br>except Reporting<br>except legacy integration<br>except bulk operations<br>except endpoint X<br>unless attribute Y exists<br>unless interface Z is implemented</code></pre><br><br>The centralized configuration eventually becomes another complex program.<br><br>The apparent simplicity of each endpoint is paid for by complexity elsewhere.<br><br>Module-level or operation-class policies may therefore scale better:<br><br><pre><code>system defaults<br>→ module defaults<br>→ operation category<br>→ explicit operation override</code></pre><br><br>For example:<br><br><pre><code>Catalog:<br>    external reads may retry<br><br>Payments:<br>    commands do not retry unless explicitly idempotent<br><br>Reporting:<br>    long timeout<br>    read-only transaction semantics</code></pre><br><br>The policy implementation remains centralized, while the semantic choice stays close to the operation. | *(End of restored note)* |
| **Abstractions Could Become Mechanically Expandable**<br><br>There is another possible solution that does not require removing existing abstractions.<br><br>Future frameworks and development tools could expose the resolved semantics of an operation.<br><br>The source might contain:<br><br><pre><code>.Retry(RetryPolicies.ExternalRead)</code></pre><br><br>while an agent can request:<br><br><pre><code>resolve RetryPolicies.ExternalRead</code></pre><br><br>and receive:<br><br><pre><code>max attempts: 3<br>backoff: exponential<br>jitter: enabled<br>retry:<br>  timeout<br>  502<br>  503<br>  504</code></pre><br><br>The same mechanism could resolve an entire endpoint:<br><br><pre><code>GetOrder<br><br>authentication:<br>    required<br><br>authorization:<br>    ReadOrderPolicy<br><br>validation:<br>    GetOrderValidator<br><br>tenant:<br>    request tenant<br><br>transaction:<br>    read-only<br><br>retry:<br>    ExternalRead<br>    attempts: 3<br><br>handler:<br>    GetOrderHandler<br><br>cache:<br>    OrderById<br>    TTL: 5 minutes</code></pre><br><br>This suggests an important property for future abstractions:<br><br>> Abstractions should be mechanically expandable.<br><br>Documentation is useful.<br><br>A machine-readable resolved execution model is much more useful to an agent. | *(End of restored note)* |
| **Good Abstractions for Agents May Optimize for Different Things**<br><br>Traditional APIs often optimize for:<br><br><pre><code>few lines<br>few parameters<br>minimal boilerplate<br>maximum reuse</code></pre><br><br>Agent-oriented APIs may increasingly optimize for:<br><br><pre><code>semantic locality<br>explicit dependencies<br>visible execution flow<br>mechanically discoverable behavior<br>predictable composition</code></pre><br><br>This does not imply that code must become low-level.<br><br>For example:<br><br><pre><code>.RetryTransient(3)</code></pre><br><br>is still an abstraction.<br><br>It hides backoff implementation, timers, exception matching, and scheduling.<br><br>But it preserves the fact that matters semantically:<br><br><pre><code>the operation may execute multiple times</code></pre><br><br>By contrast:<br><br><pre><code>.ExecuteUsingStandardEnterprisePolicies()</code></pre><br><br>may hide almost everything the agent needs to know.<br><br>A useful distinction is therefore:<br><br>> A good abstraction reduces syntax without hiding important semantics. | *(End of restored note)* |
| **Business Meaning Should Be Encoded in the Same Vocabulary**<br><br>Semantic locality is not only about where behavior executes.<br><br>It is also about whether the code uses the same concepts and vocabulary as the domain, documentation, API contracts, database schema, tests, and operational descriptions.<br><br>Consider:<br><br><pre><code>if (payment != null)<br>{<br>    ...<br>}</code></pre><br><br>In a particular system, this may implicitly mean:<br><br><pre><code>the invoice is unpaid</code></pre><br><br>A developer who has worked on the system for years may know that convention.<br><br>An agent may not.<br><br>The agent sees evidence:<br><br><pre><code>Payment exists</code></pre><br><br>but must infer the business conclusion:<br><br><pre><code>invoice is unpaid</code></pre><br><br>That inference may be correct, incorrect, or missed entirely.<br><br>Compare that with:<br><br><pre><code>if (payment.Status == PaymentStatus.Unpaid)<br>{<br>    ...<br>}</code></pre><br><br>or:<br><br><pre><code>if (payment.IsUnpaid)<br>{<br>    ...<br>}</code></pre><br><br>Now the business concept is explicitly represented in the code.<br><br>This matters particularly when the same concept appears elsewhere in the system.<br><br>Suppose the documentation says:<br><br><pre><code>retry unpaid payments</code></pre><br><br>the API specification contains:<br><br><pre><code>paymentStatus: unpaid</code></pre><br><br>and tests are named:<br><br><pre><code>ShouldRetryUnpaidPayment</code></pre><br><br>An agent searching for or reasoning about "unpaid payment" can directly associate all of these artifacts with:<br><br><pre><code>PaymentStatus.Unpaid</code></pre><br><br>It has a much weaker semantic connection to:<br><br><pre><code>payment != null</code></pre><br><br>The same problem appears with sentinel values and technical representations:<br><br><pre><code>amount == 0<br>endDate == null<br>retryCount == -1<br>status == 2<br>customerId != null</code></pre><br><br>These values may encode business meanings such as:<br><br><pre><code>free<br>active<br>unlimited retries<br>awaiting payment<br>customer assigned</code></pre><br><br>but the meaning is not present in the expression itself.<br><br>A more agent-friendly model exposes the conclusion:<br><br><pre><code>price.IsFree<br>subscription.IsActive<br>retryPolicy.IsUnlimited<br>payment.Status == PaymentStatus.Unpaid<br>order.HasAssignedCustomer</code></pre><br><br>This suggests a broader rule:<br><br>> Prefer code that encodes business conclusions rather than only technical evidence from which those conclusions must be inferred.<br><br>The principle extends beyond source code.<br><br>Ideally, the same domain vocabulary should appear consistently in:<br><br><pre><code>domain model<br>API contracts<br>database schema<br>tests<br>documentation<br>events and messages<br>logs and telemetry</code></pre><br><br>For example:<br><br><pre><code>Documentation:<br>    unpaid payment<br><br>Code:<br>    PaymentStatus.Unpaid<br><br>API:<br>    paymentStatus = &quot;unpaid&quot;<br><br>Database:<br>    payment_status = &quot;unpaid&quot;<br><br>Event:<br>    PaymentBecameUnpaid<br><br>Test:<br>    ShouldRetryUnpaidPayment</code></pre><br><br>This creates **semantic alignment across artifacts**.<br><br>For an agent, that alignment has several benefits:<br><br>&bull; repository search becomes more reliable,<br><br>&bull; embeddings and RAG retrieval are more likely to connect relevant artifacts,<br><br>&bull; documentation can be mapped to implementation more directly,<br><br>&bull; fewer hidden conventions must be reconstructed,<br><br>&bull; code review requires less inference,<br><br>&bull; generated changes are more likely to use the correct business concept.<br><br>Comments can help:<br><br><pre><code>// A non-null Payment means the invoice has not been paid yet.<br>if (payment != null)</code></pre><br><br>but comments are weaker than encoding the meaning in the model itself.<br><br>They can become stale, they may not participate in all tooling, and they still leave the underlying representation semantically indirect.<br><br>Documentation or schema descriptions are also useful when the technical representation cannot be changed.<br><br>For example, if a legacy database uses:<br><br><pre><code>payment_state = 2</code></pre><br><br>then the schema or mapping layer should make the meaning mechanically discoverable:<br><br><pre><code>2 = unpaid</code></pre><br><br>or preferably expose it to application code as:<br><br><pre><code>PaymentStatus.Unpaid</code></pre><br><br>This leads to another useful design principle:<br><br>> Use the same business vocabulary across code, contracts, schemas, tests, and documentation whenever practical.<br><br>For humans, this reduces the amount of institutional knowledge needed to understand the system.<br><br>For agents, it reduces the number of semantic translations that must be inferred before a change can be made safely.<br><br>In this sense, agent-friendly code should not merely be readable.<br><br>It should be **semantically searchable and cross-referenceable**. | *(End of restored note)* |
| **Semantic Locality May Become an Architectural Goal**<br><br>We can think about code as having different levels of semantic locality.<br><br>High semantic locality:<br><br><pre><code>CalculatePrice(order, customer, pricingRules);</code></pre><br><br>The important inputs are visible.<br><br>Lower semantic locality:<br><br><pre><code>priceCalculator.Calculate(order);</code></pre><br><br>The implementation must be discovered.<br><br>Even lower semantic locality:<br><br><pre><code>priceCalculator.Calculate(order);</code></pre><br><br>where dependency injection selects the implementation based on runtime context.<br><br>Very low semantic locality:<br><br><pre><code>priceCalculator.Calculate(order);</code></pre><br><br>where the result also depends on:<br><br><pre><code>current tenant<br>feature flags<br>ambient user<br>interceptors<br>global cache<br>transaction context<br>dynamic configuration</code></pre><br><br>The textual code can remain equally short while the reasoning cost increases dramatically.<br><br>For agent-maintained systems, **semantic locality may become as important as traditional measures such as coupling, cohesion, and duplication**. | *(End of restored note)* |
| **The Likely Direction Is Not "No Abstractions"**<br><br>The more realistic direction is:<br><br><pre><code>hide mechanisms<br>expose semantic decisions<br>centralize implementation<br>localize intent<br>make abstractions inspectable</code></pre><br><br>This could lead to code that is somewhat more verbose than today's most heavily abstracted application architectures.<br><br>But the code may also become:<br><br>&bull; easier for agents to modify,<br><br>&bull; easier for humans to review,<br><br>&bull; easier to test,<br><br>&bull; easier to analyze statically,<br><br>&bull; less dependent on institutional knowledge,<br><br>&bull; safer to refactor automatically.<br><br>The important shift may therefore not be from abstraction to no abstraction.<br><br>It may be from:<br><br><pre><code>implicit, non-local behavior</code></pre><br><br>toward:<br><br><pre><code>explicit, composable, mechanically discoverable behavior</code></pre><br><br>In software increasingly written and maintained by agents, the cost of repetition may fall while the cost of hidden semantics becomes much more visible.<br><br>That could change what we consider "clean" architecture. | *(End of restored note)* |
